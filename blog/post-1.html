What Is Cost Segregation? Basis, Timing, and Tax Benefit Explained

Published September 1, 2026 · Reviewed November 22, 2026

Cost segregation separates a property's supported costs into asset categories for depreciation. Some components may qualify for shorter recovery periods than the building. The study does not create additional purchase cost, establish an automatic percentage to reclassify, or prove that a larger deduction reduces this year's tax.

Start with three separate questions: What basis is available? Which depreciation rules apply? When can this taxpayer use the deduction? A favorable answer to one does not settle the others. Use the blank basis and implementation review record to collect the documents and unresolved decisions before requesting a savings estimate.

1. Reconcile purchase price and depreciable basis first

A lump-sum purchase of land and buildings requires an allocation. IRS Publication 551 describes allocation using relative fair market values. Land itself is not depreciable. Review the treatment of settlement costs, improvements, business use, and prior adjustments; purchase price, current appraised value, and adjusted depreciation basis are different concepts.

The following amounts are invented to demonstrate a reconciliation, not a recommended land percentage or a property valuation. Assume an all-business-use purchase with no other assets, transaction costs, improvements, credits, or basis adjustments. A $500,000 total allocated $100,000 to land leaves $400,000 for the depreciable property. A missing allocation stays unresolved. An internally inconsistent allocation must be corrected before calculating depreciation.

2. Classification needs component evidence

Under the general depreciation system, residential rental property generally has a 27.5-year recovery period and nonresidential real property 39 years. Some separately identified personal property or land improvements may qualify for shorter periods, such as 5, 7, or 15 years. The applicable system, property definition, use, and exceptions matter; an alternative depreciation system can produce different treatment. See Internal Revenue Code section 168.

A component's label alone is insufficient. Ask the preparer to connect each proposed asset group to its function, installation, cost evidence, and legal classification. Do not apply a target percentage to make a projected benefit attractive. Reconcile the identified costs to the property's supported basis without counting the same cost twice.

The IRS Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide discusses study methods, documentation, cost reconciliation, and preparer experience. It is examiner guidance, not binding law or an IRS approval of a provider. It states that preparers have no prescribed qualifications; relevant expertise and a supported methodology still deserve review. A professional title does not establish every classification in a report.

3. Apply the correct dates, method, and convention

Publication 946 explains that depreciation begins when property is ready and available for its intended use. Buying a property, commissioning a study, and placing an asset in service are different events. Residential rental and nonresidential real property use the mid-month convention. Dividing basis by 27.5 or 39 gives a full-year reference amount under straight-line assumptions, not an automatic first-year deduction. Shorter-lived assets have their own applicable methods and conventions.

IRS Topic 704 describes the restored 100% special depreciation allowance for qualified property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. This is a conditional rule, not a blanket write-off of a property purchase. Review acquisition rules, eligible assets, use, exclusions, elections, and the law applicable to the relevant year. Finishing a report in 2026 does not turn an earlier acquisition into a qualifying 2026 acquisition.

For a worked timing example, use the first-year depreciation comparison. For property already on prior returns, use the existing-property and Form 3115 review. The filing route depends on the facts and prior depreciation treatment; a study's completion date alone does not select the return year.

4. Separate a depreciation deduction from usable tax benefit

A deduction reduces the relevant tax base if allowed; it is not a dollar-for-dollar tax credit. A projected depreciation amount multiplied by a marginal rate is not a complete tax calculation. Publication 925 explains at-risk and passive-activity limits. Real estate professional status alone does not establish material participation in each relevant rental activity. Short average stays can change the rental-activity analysis but do not by themselves prove nonpassive treatment or use of a loss against wages. Other applicable limits and the taxpayer's complete return still need review.

The short-term rental guide separates the stay-length exception, participation evidence, and loss usability. Document which amount is currently usable and which amount, if any, is suspended. Leave the tax benefit unresolved when the return-level inputs are missing.

5. Decide whether further analysis is worth its cost

Request a written proposal and compare deliverables, records needed, inspection and travel charges, return implementation, and post-delivery support. The study pricing and scope guide provides fictional proposal comparisons without claiming a universal fee per square foot.

Ask your preparer to compare timing of usable deductions, fees, state treatment, planned holding period, and potential sale consequences, including applicable depreciation recapture. Earlier deductions may shift tax between years; they are not automatically permanent tax savings. Avoid a minimum property-value cutoff or a guaranteed ROI without supported facts. A documented decision to defer or decline a study is a valid outcome.

What to bring to the first review

  • Closing statement and allocation support, or construction cost records.
  • Asset descriptions, photographs or inspection records, business-use facts, and acquisition and placed-in-service dates.
  • Prior depreciation schedules, relevant elections, improvements, and basis adjustments.
  • Participation and rental records, carryovers, and information needed for the preparer's return-level analysis.
  • Written study and implementation proposals plus your holding-period assumptions.

The blank review record separates each input from its source, reviewer, and review date. It does not calculate a tax result. Preserve unknowns and record the next question rather than treating an unverified estimate as a fact.
