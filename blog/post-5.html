Cost Segregation Study Cost: Compare Scope, Fees, and Missing Items

The useful price for a cost segregation study is the cost of the defined work your property and tax preparer need. A headline fee alone does not establish that cost, the quality of the analysis, or the amount of tax benefit. Obtain a written proposal with deliverables, exclusions, assumptions, payment terms, and named responsibilities.

This guide does not quote current market rates or promise a return on the fee. Use the blank quote review record and fictional scope-adjusted fee comparison to compare proposals without treating missing charges as zero.

Define the property and scope before comparing fees

Give each provider the same property list, ownership and use facts, acquisition or construction dates, available plans and invoices, existing depreciation records, and the intended reporting year. Identify multiple buildings, improvements, furnishings, prior studies, missing records, and any expected sale or transfer. A fee based on one building’s records is not automatically a quote for the whole portfolio.

Ask what evidence the provider will collect and how. Record any site visit, travel, photo or document review, record reconstruction, cost-estimation method, asset schedule, basis reconciliation, legal classification explanation, reviewer, and delivery date. Specify which assumptions will require confirmation and what happens if the records change the scope.

Separate the study report from implementation

Confirm who reconciles the report with the existing fixed-asset ledger, reviews owner-level loss limits, prepares any Form 3115 or other required filing, updates depreciation schedules, and addresses state differences. These tasks may be performed by different people and charged separately. Report delivery does not prove that tax implementation is complete.

For post-delivery support, define the service, time allowance, duration, response process, and additional hourly or project fees. A phrase such as “audit support included” needs a scope: answering a preparer’s question, supplying source documents, making a report correction, and representing a taxpayer are different tasks. Obtain the actual terms rather than assigning them a dollar value from a marketing label.

Three fictional proposal comparisons

Assume the same invented 3,000-square-foot property and a comparison scope consisting of a study report, one site visit with travel, a separately estimated preparer implementation task, and one specified hour of post-delivery support. All amounts below are invented, no additional charges or taxes are assumed for cases A and B, and inclusion is assumed only where shown. These are arithmetic exercises, not vendor quotes, market averages, recommended fees, or findings that the deliverables are adequate.

Proposal A’s $1,200 + $800 + $600 + $400 equals $3,000. Proposal B’s $2,400 + $600 also equals $3,000. This comparison does not establish equivalent quality or recommend either provider. Proposal C’s $2,400 is a known subtotal, not a completed total. Its visit and support charges require answers before comparing the total or dividing it by square footage.

The $1.00-per-square-foot figure is a description of these invented totals and this supplied denominator. It is not a published market rate, a minimum property-value test, or evidence that a different property should cost the same. Check how each proposal measures area and includes multiple buildings before using a unit-price comparison.

Examine the evidence behind the report

The IRS Cost Segregation Audit Technique Guide, Publication 5653, discusses study approaches and quality elements. It is examiner guidance, not a binding statement of law or an IRS approval program. Its discussion supports asking how costs, assumptions, classifications, and total basis can be traced and reconciled.

Ask for the preparer’s relevant construction, cost-estimating, and tax experience, the methodology, an appropriately redacted sample report, and the review process. The guide notes that there are no prescribed preparer qualifications in its discussion; a professional designation by itself does not guarantee an accepted result. Supported estimates and actual cost records need their own explanation. A report’s price alone does not establish its reliability.

Read any percentage-of-benefit fee formula carefully: identify the benefit definition, assumed tax rate, deduction versus actual tax cash, floor or cap, payment trigger, and adjustment process. The audit guide raises concerns about incentives in contingency arrangements. Do not describe such a formula as automatically aligning everyone’s interests or guaranteeing compliance. Have the relevant professionals review the proposed arrangement and applicable rules.

Keep tax treatment and project cost separate

The fee’s tax treatment depends on the facts, purpose of the expenditure, capitalization rules, accounting method, and applicable limitations. It is not established merely by labeling the invoice a study fee or paying it in a particular year. Sections 162 and 263 illustrate why expense and capital-treatment questions require review. This article does not assign a current deduction or an after-tax fee reduction.

A projected accelerated deduction also is not the incremental tax benefit from the study. Compare supported whole-return results, deductions available without the study, loss usability, future depreciation, state treatment, sale or recapture effects, study and implementation fees, and timing. The first-year comparison, existing-property method-change review, and STR classification review address separate parts of that work.

Questions to resolve before accepting a proposal

  1. Which property, buildings, improvements, and reporting years does the written fee cover?
  2. Which records, inspection work, assumptions, schedules, and review deliverables are included?
  3. Who performs and charges for the return implementation, reconciliations, and later support?
  4. Which costs remain unknown, and what written change-order, cancellation, and payment terms apply?
  5. Has the tax preparer separately evaluated eligibility, usable benefit, fee treatment, and future consequences?

Record the proposal version and date, resolve exclusions in writing, and save the final agreed scope with the report and implementation records. A completed comparison organizes a buying decision; it does not establish a provider’s work quality or the owner’s deduction.

Primary sources and review date

  • IRS Publication 5653, Cost Segregation Audit Technique Guide, February 2025: methodology, report quality, preparer experience, and fee-arrangement review.
  • IRS Publication 946: depreciation eligibility and implementation context.
  • 26 USC 162 and 26 USC 263: expense and capital-expenditure framework.
