Cost Segregation Records and Report Review: A Practical Checklist

Read a cost segregation study from its supporting records toward the depreciation summary. Start with the property and cost history, check the asset detail and assumptions, and then give the tax preparer a file that explains what is known and what remains unresolved. A report total alone does not establish a currently usable deduction or a tax saving.

This guide brings the records and study-quality review into one sequence. Download the blank records and report-review register to assign missing documents and decisions. It is an organizing tool, not a study certification or tax calculation.

1. Establish which property and costs the report covers

Compare the engagement and report with the legal owner, property address, acquisition documents, construction history, and improvement periods. Ask whether separately purchased furnishings, tenant work, later additions, and prior studies are included. An address match does not prove that two reports cover the same costs or period.

IRS Publication 551 explains basis and allocation of a lump-sum purchase between land and buildings. Do not substitute the current property value for the supported tax basis or count the same cost in both a purchase allocation and a separate asset schedule. Use the basis and depreciation introduction for the underlying concepts.

3. Review study support, not an advertised percentage

The IRS Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide discusses preparer experience, methodology, documentation, asset numbering, legal analysis, and reconciliation of allocated costs to actual costs. Use those topics to ask focused questions about the report. The guide is examiner guidance, not binding law or an IRS approval of a provider.

It states that there are no prescribed qualifications for preparers. Relevant experience and a supported method matter, but a professional title or a claim to follow the guide does not settle every classification. Ask the provider to explain material assumptions, unsupported estimates, and differences from the supplied records. No particular reclassification percentage proves report quality or audit acceptance.

For a proposal rather than a completed study, the study pricing and scope guide helps separate report preparation, inspections, implementation, and post-delivery support.

4. Resolve duplicate and missing evidence explicitly

In a fictional review file, a purchase allocation includes furnishings while the draft study also lists a furnishing invoice separately. The reviewer cannot assume that the invoice represents an additional cost. Ask whether it describes an asset already included, a replacement, or a genuinely separate acquisition, and obtain the supporting reconciliation.

If the relationship is unknown, mark it unresolved. Do not add the invoice merely because it is available, subtract an unsupported estimate to make totals agree, or treat a missing amount as zero. This example supplies no dollar amounts and establishes no depreciable basis or deduction.

5. Give the preparer a complete implementation handoff

Publication 946 explains depreciation methods, periods, conventions, and placed-in-service rules. A report date is not itself a placed-in-service date. Ask the preparer to identify the applicable year and rules, check prior reporting, and document the treatment used on the return.

  • Keep the final signed report and the version of the source records used.
  • Identify unresolved classification, ownership, date, and cost questions before relying on the result.
  • Provide the prior depreciation history for the existing-property and Form 3115 review; do not assume a filing route from the study date.
  • Separate calculated depreciation from the deduction currently usable on the taxpayer's return. The first-year comparison and STR participation and loss-use guide address different parts of that review.

Record who prepares the returns, who updates the asset register, and who answers later questions. A study provider's deliverable and the preparer's filing work should be identified separately in the engagement.

6. Retain the continuing property history

Publication 583 says to retain property records until the limitations period expires for the year of taxable disposition. A routine three-year purge after buying a property is not a substitute for that rule. Keep the acquisition and improvement evidence, depreciation history, and later sale or disposition records together. Other legal, insurance, or lender requirements may require longer retention.

Use a stable document reference and asset identifier so that a future preparer can follow changes. The blank register has space for the document reference, asset or cost identifier, report page, question, reviewer, next action, and review date. Nothing in it is pre-approved or populated with assumed amounts.

General education. The duplicate-record example is fictional. This checklist does not establish a property allocation, study acceptance, filing eligibility, usable loss, or tax saving.
